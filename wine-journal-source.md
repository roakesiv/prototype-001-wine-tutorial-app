## 3. Core Product Philosophy from the Journal

### Main framing
Wine feels large, complex, and socially intimidating because of:
- huge shelves and lists,
- strange terms,
- snobby culture,
- overwhelm for beginners.

The journal's purpose is to demystify wine and simplify the experience while helping the learner build knowledge, appreciation, and practical tasting skill.

### Product principles pulled from the journal
- Learning wine should feel fun and practical.
- The tool should be active and used while drinking wine.
- Beginners should not be intimidated by dense-looking references.
- Practice and reflection matter more than memorization.
- The learner can improve quickly by focusing on fundamentals first.
- System thinking is more useful than trying to memorize everything.

### Strong product takeaway for Prototype 02
The app should feel like a scaffold for a beginner wine amateur:
- supportive,
- active,
- practical,
- low-snobbery,
- focused on confidence through guided repetition.

## 5. Teaching Philosophy and Learning Framework

### High-level teaching framework
The journal uses a scaffold model:
- give the learner essential reference material,
- let them practice while tasting,
- use repeated structured notes,
- encourage reflection,
- build skill over time.

### Learning loop implied by the journal
1. Taste the wine.
2. Try to describe it from memory.
3. Use references to fill in missing pieces.
4. Write notes.
5. Repeat over time.

### Product implication
Prototype 02 should not try to teach everything at once.
It should:
- teach just enough at each step,
- encourage action,
- reinforce through repetition,
- make progress feel manageable.

## 6. Direct vs Indirect Description

### Direct description
Direct description is the beginner-friendly base.
It focuses on reasonably objective and limited features such as:
- look,
- smell,
- taste,
- body / mouthfeel.

It is concise and easier to learn quickly.

### Indirect description
Indirect description is more subjective, marketing-like, embellished, and harder for beginners to use confidently.

### Teaching implication
Prototype 02 should teach direct description first.
Indirect description can be framed as a later layer or optional flourish.

### Candidate lesson snippets
- Start with direct descriptions. They are simpler and easier to trust.
- You do not need poetic wine language to begin tasting well.
- First describe what you notice. Stylish language can come later.

### Useful app implication
The app can teach:
- direct tasting skill first,
- personal reflection second,
- polished wine language later.

## 7. Power Scale and Structured Assessment

### Core idea
The journal emphasizes “power scales” for features like:
- color,
- flavor / aroma intensity,
- taste,
- body.

The point is to reduce complexity and make description more concrete.

### Teaching implication
Structured scales help beginners:
- make a decision,
- reduce vague language,
- build confidence,
- compare wines more easily.

### Product implication
Prototype 02 can keep structured choices, but the lesson text should explain:
- why the choice matters,
- how to think about it simply,
- that “good enough” judgments are fine.

## 8. Step-by-Step Tasting Mechanics

### Overall tasting flow from the journal
The journal describes a six-step technical tasting flow:
1. look,
2. swirl,
3. sniff,
4. sip,
5. swish,
6. savor.

### Product implication
The app does not need to expose all six technical actions as major steps if that hurts usability.
But it should preserve the spirit:
- observe,
- smell,
- taste,
- feel,
- reflect.

## 9. Step-Specific Source Content for the App

## 9.1 Welcome / Before You Start

### Source themes
- Wine can feel intimidating because of culture and language.
- The learner should not wait to become “an expert” before participating.
- The best way to learn is to taste, write, reflect, and repeat.
- The goal is practical confidence, not perfection.

### Candidate lesson snippets
- Wine can seem complicated, but the basics are learnable.
- The goal here is not to sound fancy. It is to notice what you experience.
- You can build skill one glass at a time.

---

## 9.2 Look

### Strong source material
- Tilt the glass over a white surface to reduce background noise.
- Identify hue: white, rosé, or red.
- Notice color depth / intensity.
- Look for signs of aging or browning.
- Color intensity can hint at flavor intensity.

### Candidate lesson note
Start with what you can see. Hold the glass over a light or white background and notice the color and how deep it looks. You do not need to know everything yet—just begin with a clear visual observation.

### Candidate microcopy
- Start with color. It is the easiest place to begin.
- Look for hue first, then depth.
- A simple visual note is enough to get started.

---

## 9.3 Swirl and Smell

### Strong source material
- Swirling increases surface area and boosts aroma intensity.
- Smell is the main sense in tasting.
- Take two to three deep sniffs.
- Notice intensity and the main aroma family.
- Aroma can be grouped broadly before getting specific.

### Broad aroma groupings from the journal
- floral,
- red fruit,
- dried fruit,
- spicy,
- earthy.

### Candidate lesson note
Swirling helps the wine release aroma. Take a few deep sniffs and ask a simple question first: what broad family stands out most—fruit, floral, herbal, earthy, or spice?

### Candidate microcopy
- Start broad before trying to get specific.
- You do not need the perfect aroma word. Pick the family that feels closest.
- Smell is one of the biggest parts of tasting.

---

## 9.4 Taste

### Strong source material
- Take a larger sip.
- Hold it for a few seconds.
- Coat the mouth.
- Start by assessing sweetness and acidity.
- Direct descriptions are easier for beginners than poetic descriptions.

### Specific source ideas
- Sweetness is the sugary sensation on the tongue.
- Acidity creates a sour / salivating reaction.

### Candidate lesson note
When you taste, begin with the easiest structured questions: does it feel dry or sweet, and does it feel more soft or more tangy? You are not trying to sound technical—you are just learning to notice patterns.

### Candidate microcopy
- Start with dry vs sweet.
- Then notice whether the wine feels soft or tangy.
- Simple choices are enough for a strong beginner note.

---

## 9.5 Feel / Mouthfeel

### Strong source material
- Swishing increases contact and makes taste and mouthfeel more vivid.
- Body can be understood as weight or texture.
- Tannin is the drying, astringent feeling from skins, seeds, and stems.
- Finish is about how sensations linger after swallowing.

### Useful analogies from the journal
- Light body = skim milk
- Medium body = chocolate milk
- Heavy body = milkshake

### Candidate lesson note
This step is about how the wine feels, not just what it tastes like. Notice body, drying sensation, and how long the flavors last after you swallow.

### Candidate microcopy
- Think about weight and texture here.
- Tannin often feels drying.
- Finish is simply how long the impression lasts.

---

## 9.6 Savor / Reflect

### Strong source material
- Aftertastes can linger for a while.
- This is the moment for personal judgment as well as sensory reflection.
- The journal acknowledges both technical tasting and more holistic “total feel” approaches.

### Product implication
The final step does not need to be purely technical.
It can include:
- reaction,
- preference,
- simple summary,
- personal reflection.

### Candidate lesson note
After the technical pieces, pause and ask the personal question: what did this wine feel like overall, and would you want it again?

### Candidate microcopy
- You are allowed to have a personal reaction.
- Technical notes help, but your overall impression matters too.
- Reflection is part of learning.

## 10. Beginner Learning Path from the Journal

### Strong source sequence
1. Learn the basic tasting mechanics.
2. Use direct, objective features first.
3. Build direct descriptions from those observations.
4. Add aroma and adjectives later.
5. Practice every time you taste.

### Product implication
Prototype 02 should likely teach in that exact order:
- simple observation first,
- complexity later.

### Candidate lesson sequence for the app
- Step 1: easy visual observation
- Step 2: broad smell family
- Step 3: simple taste structure
- Step 4: mouthfeel and finish
- Step 5: personal + structured reflection

## 11. Tasting Note Guidance

### Strong source ideas

- basic wine info,
- analysis,
- faults,
- conclusions.

The most product-relevant parts for the current app are:
- analysis,
- conclusions,
- personal scoring,
- direct vs indirect note writing.

## 12. Tone Guidance

### Strong tone from the journal
The strongest tone signals are:
- demystifying,
- anti-snobbery,
- encouraging,
- practical,
- field-ready,
- amused rather than precious,
- growth-oriented.

### Tone rules for app copy
Use:
- short, encouraging instructions,
- permission-giving language,
- practical guidance,
- friendly phrasing,
- confidence-building reminders.

Avoid:
- stiff classroom language,
- wine snob language,
- long dense paragraphs,
- pressure-heavy instructions,
- overuse of specialist terms without explanation.

### Candidate tone lines
- Start simple.
- Good enough is good enough here.
- You are building a skill, not taking a test.
- Pick the closest answer and keep moving.
- You can always refine later.

## 14. Content That Should Be Reviewed Before Shipping

The journal is very useful, but some statements appear broad, simplified, or draft-like. These should be reviewed before being used as authoritative tutorial copy.

Examples of items to review or nuance:
- “Whites have no tannins.”
- “Oak is for whites only.”
- “More sun = less acidity, more aroma, tannin, and body.”
- any broad region generalizations that may be too compressed for teaching without qualification.

These can still be useful as thinking prompts, but they should not be copied directly into product content without review.
